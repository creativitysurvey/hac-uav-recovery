\title{Simple Hybrids, Smooth Schedules: Rethinking Adaptive Fusion \\
for Resilient UAV Swarm Connectivity Recovery}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Self-healing connectivity recovery for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms
requires balancing two competing objectives after partial node failure:
restoring network connectivity as quickly as possible, and preserving wide
communication coverage. \citet{peng2026r2c} recently proposed R2C, a
resilient recovery framework that divides this problem into an
intra-subnetwork virtual-force expansion module, a subnetwork-level
multipartite graph convolution module, and a time-adaptive fusion strategy
(AWF) that blends the two. In this work, we systematically ablate the
design space that R2C occupies along three axes -- decision paradigm
(heuristic / learned / hybrid), spatial reasoning granularity (node-level /
subnetwork-level / global-level), and connectivity--coverage trade-off
strategy (fixed / staged / adaptive) -- through a sequence of controlled
experiments. We show that a markedly simpler hybrid architecture, combining
a parameter-free virtual-force branch with a single global graph
convolution branch fused at the velocity level, matches or exceeds the
robustness of considerably more complex learned baselines, and retains
$88$--$91\%$ of R2C's reported coverage under $80\%$ node failure despite
omitting R2C's boundary-node extraction and multipartite graph
construction. We further show that naively porting R2C's adaptive fusion
schedule to this simplified architecture degrades robustness rather than
improving it, trace the failure to the steepness (not the timing) of the
fusion weight's transition, and both empirically and theoretically explain
why: the transition schedule acts as an implicit training curriculum for
the learned branch, and a steep transition forces the network to learn
under a harder, less forgiving regime. We validate every finding across two
deployment scenarios and five random seeds, and additionally report a
falsified hypothesis and a documented numerical bug fix as part of an
honest account of the research process. Code and full experimental logs are
released to support reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem and Motivation}

\subsubsection{The UAV Swarm Self-Healing Connectivity Recovery Problem}
Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms are an increasingly attractive
platform for rapid, flexible wireless communication in settings such as
post-disaster relief, dynamic battlefields, and temporarily congested
areas \citep{coletta2023auav,shi2025symmetry,wang2022task,wang2024angle}. A recurring
operational risk in these deployments is \emph{partial node failure}:
internal faults or external attacks can remove a subset of UAVs from the
swarm at any time \citep{wang2024survey,kurunathan2023machine}. When enough
nodes fail, the remaining swarm's communication graph can fracture into
several disconnected subnetworks, and both the quality of the communication
service and the spatial coverage of the mission area degrade sharply
\citep{li2023reinforcement,xu2022safebench,peng2022smarttro}. The
\emph{self-healing communication connectivity} (SCC) problem asks how to
reposition the surviving UAVs, using only local or partially-observed
information, so that (i) the swarm's communication graph becomes fully
connected again as quickly as possible, and (ii) the swarm continues to
provide as much spatial coverage as possible while doing so.

\subsubsection{The Inherent Tension Between Connectivity and Coverage}
These two objectives are in direct tension. Pulling surviving nodes closer
together accelerates reconnection but shrinks the covered area; spreading
nodes out preserves coverage but slows -- or in the worst case prevents --
reconnection. Managing this trade-off, especially as the fraction of failed
nodes grows large, is the central technical difficulty of the SCC problem,
and is the difficulty that motivates essentially every method surveyed in
Section~\ref{sec:related-work}.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Approaches}

\subsubsection{Heuristic Methods Do Not Scale}
Non-learning SCC methods rely on hand-designed rules: identifying and
compensating for critical nodes
\citep{akkaya2009distributed,mi2015obstacle,zhang2024uavenabled}, or
guiding surviving nodes toward predefined rendezvous
points \citep{joshi2012autonomous,shriwastav2018roundtable,chen2020sidr,wang2024apfcpp}.
These methods are conceptually simple and require no training data, but
they depend on static assumptions and hand-crafted coordination rules that
make them brittle under large-scale, dynamic, or previously unseen failure
patterns \citep{tosun2024dpkcr}.

\subsubsection{Learned Methods Suffer from Spatial Collapse}
Graph neural network (GNN) based methods
\citep{mou2022resilient,peng2025prioritized,wang2025uav} recast SCC as a
learned graph optimization problem and have demonstrated faster recovery
and stronger adaptability than heuristic baselines. However, these methods
are prone to \emph{spatial collapse}: GNN aggregation biases surviving UAVs
to concentrate around high-degree nodes, producing an unbalanced spatial
distribution and substantial coverage loss. Diffusion-based and
differential-subgraph-based methods
\citep{lin2024multihop,lin2025multihop} partially alleviate this by
encouraging spatially distributed recovery, but still struggle to achieve a
stable, balanced layout under dynamic conditions, and generally do not
model real-time collision avoidance.

\subsection{Our Approach and Findings}

\subsubsection{A Natural Question Raised by R2C's Architecture}
\citet{peng2026r2c} address this connectivity--coverage trade-off with
R2C, which combines a buffered virtual-force field for intra-subnetwork
expansion (ISE) with a multipartite graph convolution module for
inter-subnetwork translation (IST), fused with a time-adaptive weighting
scheme (AWF). R2C reports strong empirical results, but its design bundles
together several independent choices -- \emph{should} the learned
component operate at subnetwork granularity rather than globally, and
\emph{does} the adaptive fusion schedule need the specific sigmoid
parameters R2C reports, or would any reasonable adaptive schedule do? These
questions are not addressed by R2C's own ablations, which toggle whole
modules on and off rather than isolating the granularity and
weighting-schedule axes independently.

\subsubsection{Summary of Findings}
We answer these questions with a sequence of controlled experiments
(Sections~\ref{sec:motivating}--\ref{sec:ablation}) built around a
deliberately simplified hybrid architecture we call \textbf{HAC} (Hybrid
Adaptive Convolution): a parameter-free virtual-force branch identical in
form to R2C's ISE, fused at the velocity level with a single \emph{global}
graph convolution branch -- i.e., explicitly \emph{without} R2C's
subnetwork partitioning, boundary-node extraction, or multipartite graph
construction. Our central findings are:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item A purely global learned branch, when fused with the heuristic
  branch under a \emph{fixed} $50/50$ weighting, already recovers
  $88$--$91\%$ of R2C's reported coverage under $80\%$ node failure, and
  does so with substantially lower variance than any single-branch
  baseline we test (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Naively porting R2C's adaptive fusion schedule (Eq.~22 in
  \citet{peng2026r2c}) to this simplified architecture \emph{degrades}
  robustness, reversing the direction of R2C's own reported ablation
  (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item The cause is not \emph{when} the fusion weight shifts control to
  the learned branch, but \emph{how steeply} it does so; smoothing the
  transition (without changing its timing) restores most of the lost
  robustness (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), and we give a semi-formal
  account of why in terms of an implicit training curriculum
  (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
  \item This fix does \emph{not} transfer to a subnetwork-level learned
  branch with rigid intra-subnetwork translation, which fails
  catastrophically under high failure rates regardless of schedule
  smoothness -- cleanly separating a \emph{schedule} failure mode from an
  \emph{architectural expressiveness} failure mode
  (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item A three-axis taxonomy of the SCC design space (decision paradigm,
  spatial granularity, trade-off strategy) that positions R2C and thirteen
  prior methods, and identifies the specific combination R2C occupies as
  previously unexplored (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}).
  \item HAC, a hybrid architecture that is substantially simpler than R2C
  along the spatial-granularity axis, together with five-seed statistical
  evidence across two deployment scenarios that it matches or exceeds
  every single-branch baseline we test, with markedly lower variance
  (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:results}).
  \item A diagnosis of why R2C's adaptive fusion schedule fails when
  transplanted into a simplified architecture, together with a validated
  fix (smoothing the schedule) and a semi-formal implicit-curriculum
  explanation with an explicit, falsifiable prediction for future work
  (Sections~\ref{sec:theory}, \ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item A fully transparent account of the negative results, a falsified
  hypothesis, and a numerical implementation bug encountered during this
  study, together with released code and experimental logs, in the belief
  that this transparency is itself useful to practitioners building
  similar hybrid recovery systems (Section~\ref{sec:discussion},
  Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Non-Learning Self-Healing Methods}

\subsubsection{Critical-Node Compensation}
One line of non-learning work identifies critical nodes -- those whose
failure disconnects the network -- and restores connectivity through
cascaded movements of their neighbors
\citep{abbasi2008movement,akkaya2009distributed,mi2015obstacle,zhang2024uavenabled}. These
methods can be effective for small, targeted failures, but the search for
critical nodes and the coordination of cascaded repositioning both incur
computational costs that scale poorly with swarm size, limiting their use
to small-scale failure scenarios.

\subsubsection{Rendezvous-Point Aggregation}
A second line of work sidesteps explicit critical-node identification by
guiding surviving nodes or entire subnetworks toward predefined rendezvous
points. Representative instances include Coulomb-force-inspired motion
planning \citep{joshi2012autonomous}, negotiation-based coordination
\citep{shriwastav2018roundtable}, and artificial-potential-field-based
aggregation \citep{chen2020sidr,wang2024apfcpp}. These methods are
conceptually simple and require no training, but rely on static assumptions
and hand-crafted coordination rules that generalize poorly to dynamic,
uncertain failure patterns \citep{tosun2024dpkcr}.

\subsection{Learning-Based Self-Healing Methods}

\subsubsection{GNN-Based Relaxed and Attention Models}
The SCC problem can be posed as a graph optimization problem, motivating
the use of GNNs to learn recovery strategies directly from data
\citep{peng2025prioritized,karalias2020erdos}. \citet{mou2022resilient}
model the damaged topology with a relaxed graph convolutional network over
virtual communication links; \citet{guo2024intelligent} extend this with
spatiotemporal attention. These methods achieve faster recovery and
stronger adaptability than heuristic baselines, but are prone to
\emph{spatial collapse}: GNN aggregation biases cause UAVs to
over-concentrate around high-degree nodes, producing an unbalanced spatial
distribution and substantial coverage loss -- a failure mode we reproduce
directly in Section~\ref{sec:motivating}.

\subsubsection{Diffusion- and Differential-Subgraph-Based Methods}
To mitigate spatial collapse, more recent work incorporates
diffusion-based propagation \citep{lin2024multihop} or bipartite
convolution over multi-hop differential subgraphs
\citep{lin2025multihop}, both of which explicitly encourage spatially
distributed recovery. These approaches improve spatial balance but still
struggle to maintain global stability under dynamic conditions and
generally overlook real-time collision avoidance, limiting practical
deployment.

\subsection{Hybrid Heuristic--Learning Architectures}

\subsubsection{R2C: Divide-and-Conquer with Adaptive Fusion}
\citet{peng2026r2c} propose R2C, which combines a buffered dynamic virtual
force field for intra-subnetwork expansion (ISE) -- categorizing pairwise
distances into repulsive, neutral, and attractive zones -- with a
multipartite graph convolution module for inter-subnetwork translation
(IST), which extracts convex-hull boundary nodes per fractured subgraph
\citep{barber1996quickhull} and constructs virtual cross-subgraph edges to
predict subnetwork-level translation vectors via a first-order Chebyshev
spectral graph convolution \citep{liu2025asymmetric}. The two branches are
combined through an adaptive weighted fusion (AWF) mechanism: a
sigmoid-shaped, time-dependent weighting function \citep{elfwing2018sigmoid}
that shifts control from the local expansion branch to the global
translation branch as recovery progresses. This work is, to our knowledge,
the first to explicitly fuse a local virtual-force field with a global
graph-convolution-based translation mechanism for SCC, and reports the
strongest empirical results among the methods we survey.

\subsubsection{Other Hybrid Recovery and Coordination Methods}
\citet{li2025fast} combine distributed multi-agent reinforcement learning
(MADDPG) with an artificial potential field term, but apply the hybridization
at node-level granularity throughout, without R2C's subnetwork-level
translation branch or its adaptive fusion schedule. \citet{wang2025uav}
apply graph-based deep reinforcement learning to topology self-healing
without an explicit heuristic component. More broadly, related coordination
and coverage problems in multi-robot systems
\citep{mutzari2025heterogeneous,ye2022multiuav,shi2025symmetry} and
graph neural network research more generally
\citep{kipf2016semi,zeng2023substructure,liu2025asymmetric} inform the
architectural choices available at each layer of the design space we
formalize next.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
\label{sec:positioning}

\subsubsection{A Three-Axis Taxonomy}
The methods surveyed above differ along (at least) three largely
independent axes: the \emph{decision paradigm} used to generate motion
commands (heuristic, learned, or hybrid), the \emph{spatial reasoning
granularity} at which that paradigm operates (node-level, subnetwork-level,
or global-level), and the \emph{connectivity--coverage trade-off strategy}
used to reconcile the two competing objectives (fixed, staged, or
adaptive). We formalize this taxonomy in
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} and use it to organize both prior work and our
own experiments.

\subsubsection{Where This Work Sits}
R2C occupies the \emph{hybrid} $\times$ \{\emph{node-level},
\emph{subnetwork-level}\} $\times$ \emph{adaptive} region of this taxonomy
-- to our knowledge the first work to do so. Its own ablations
(\emph{w/o ISE}, \emph{w/o IST}, \emph{w/o AWF}) toggle entire modules but
do not independently vary the \emph{granularity} of the learned branch or
the \emph{steepness} of the adaptive schedule. This paper fills exactly
that gap: Sections~\ref{sec:motivating}--\ref{sec:ablation} isolate each
axis in turn, starting from single-branch baselines
(Section~\ref{sec:motivating}), through a deliberately simplified hybrid
architecture (Section~\ref{sec:method}), to a systematic study of the
fusion schedule itself (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\section{Preliminaries and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{UAV Swarm Network Model}

\subsubsection{Temporal Graph Representation and Connectivity}
Following \citet{peng2026r2c}, at each discrete time step $t$ the UAV swarm
is modeled as a temporal graph $G_t = (V_t, E_t, P_t)$, where
$V_t = \{v_{1,t}, \dots, v_{|V_t|,t}\}$ is the set of surviving UAVs,
$E_t$ is the set of communication links, and
$P_t = \{\mathbf{p}_{1,t}, \dots, \mathbf{p}_{|V_t|,t}\}$ denotes their
planar positions. Let $\mathbf{A}_t$ be the adjacency matrix and
$\mathbf{L}_t = \mathbf{D}_t - \mathbf{A}_t$ the graph Laplacian. The
number of connected components $C_t$ equals the algebraic multiplicity of
the zero eigenvalue of $\mathbf{L}_t$; the swarm is fully connected iff
$C_t = 1$. Partial node failures may fracture $G_t$ into $C_t > 1$
disconnected subnetworks, which are the object of the recovery process we
study.

\subsubsection{Communication Range and Coverage}
A direct link exists between $v_i$ and $v_j$ iff their Euclidean distance
$d_{ij,t} = \|\mathbf{p}_{i,t} - \mathbf{p}_{j,t}\|_2$ does not exceed the
maximum communication range $R_c$. Following \citet{peng2026r2c}, the
inter-UAV channel is modeled as an air-to-air link
\citep{ma2019wideband,wang2025flight}, and $R_c$ is derived from a
free-space path-loss model \citep{ni2024path}. Each UAV additionally provides circular
coverage of radius $R_{\mathrm{cov}}$; the swarm's overall coverage ratio
at time $t$ is $C_{\mathrm{cov}}(t) = |\bigcup_i \mathcal{C}_i(t)| / |\mathcal{A}|$,
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the mission area and $\mathcal{C}_i(t)$ is UAV
$v_i$'s covered disk.

\subsection{Problem Definition}

\subsubsection{Recovery Time and the Optimization Objective}
Let $t_1$ be the time of failure and $t_r$ the earliest subsequent time at
which $C_{t_r} = 1$. The recovery time ratio is
$T_{\mathrm{rec}} = (t_r - t_1)/T$ for a fixed horizon $T$. Given initial
UAV states and failure dynamics, the SCC problem asks for a velocity
control policy $\hat{V}_t = \{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{1,t}, \dots,
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{|V_t|,t}\}$ minimizing
\begin{equation}
  J_c = T_{\mathrm{rec}} + \bigl(1 - C_{\mathrm{cov}}(t_r)\bigr).
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Constraints}
Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} is minimized subject to: (C1) eventual
connectivity, $C_{t_r} = 1$; (C2) velocity and position limits,
$\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i,t}\| \le \hat{v}_{\max}$ with $\mathbf{p}_{i,t} \in
\mathcal{A}$; (C3) explicit position-update dynamics,
$\mathbf{p}_{i,t} = \mathbf{p}_{i,t_1} + \sum_{t_1 \le k \le T} \Delta t
\cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i,k}$; and (C4) pairwise communication feasibility,
$d_{ij,t} \le R_c$ for any link the policy relies on. Objective
\eqref{eq:objective} makes explicit the tension we study throughout this
paper: $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$ is minimized by pulling nodes together, while
$1 - C_{\mathrm{cov}}(t_r)$ is minimized by spreading them apart.

\subsection{A Taxonomy of the Design Space}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We organize the space of SCC methods -- and our own experiments -- along
three axes that we argue are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive of
the design choices a method must make.

\subsubsection{Axis 1: Decision Paradigm}
A method's motion-command generator is either \textbf{heuristic}
(hand-designed physical or graph-theoretic rules with no learnable
parameters), \textbf{learned} (a parametric model trained on data or via
differentiable simulation), or \textbf{hybrid} (an explicit fusion of a
heuristic and a learned component). These three cases are exhaustive: any
method's core motion generator is driven by rules alone, by a trained model
alone, or by an explicit combination of both.

\subsubsection{Axis 2: Spatial Reasoning Granularity}
A method reasons about, and produces motion commands at, one of three
spatial scales: \textbf{node-level} (each UAV acts on local neighbor
information), \textbf{subnetwork-level} (each connected component of the
post-failure graph is treated as the unit of reasoning and produces a
single coordinated action), or \textbf{global-level} (the entire damaged
swarm is reasoned about jointly, without being restricted to local
neighborhoods or a subnetwork partition).

\subsubsection{Axis 3: Connectivity--Coverage Trade-off Strategy}
Finally, a method resolves the tension in Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} through
a \textbf{fixed} weighting (a constant trade-off, chosen once, between
connectivity- and coverage-oriented behavior), a \textbf{staged} strategy
(connectivity and coverage objectives are pursued in a strict temporal
sequence), or an \textbf{adaptive} strategy (the trade-off is continuously
re-weighted as a function of the recovery state or elapsed time). R2C's AWF
mechanism and our own fusion schedule (Section~\ref{sec:method}) both fall
in this last category.

These three axes define a $3 \times 3 \times 3$ space of candidate SCC
designs. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} positions R2C and the prior work surveyed
in Section~\ref{sec:related-work} within this space; cells marked

Sections~\ref{sec:motivating}--\ref{sec:ablation}.

\section{Motivating Experiments: Failure Modes of Single-Branch Designs}
\label{sec:motivating}

Before introducing our hybrid architecture, we establish empirically that
purely global and purely subnetwork-level \emph{learned} branches each fail
in a distinct, complementary way. These two architectures also serve as
baselines throughout the rest of the paper.

\subsection{Global-Level Learned Policy (GAC)}
\label{sec:gac}

\subsubsection{Architecture and Training}
\textbf{GAC} (Global Adaptive-weighted Convolution) is a single graph
convolutional policy operating over a $k$-nearest-neighbor graph built on
\emph{all} surviving UAVs in Euclidean space (not restricted to nodes
within communication range $R_c$, so that information can propagate across
disconnected subnetworks). The policy predicts a per-node 2D velocity
directly; there is no subnetwork partition and no separate node/subnetwork
branches to fuse. GAC is trained end-to-end via a differentiable multi-step
rollout with a connectivity surrogate loss, a coverage surrogate loss, and
a movement-cost loss (the same loss family used for HAC's learned branch;
see Section~\ref{sec:objective}).

\subsubsection{Results: Spatial Collapse and Low Success Rate}
Table~\ref{tab:gac-sac-random} (columns 2--3) reports GAC's connectivity
recovery success rate (fraction of 50 trials, across 5 random seeds, in
which full connectivity is restored within the recovery horizon $T$) and
mean node degree (AND, a proxy for spatial clustering) under the random
deployment scenario. GAC's success rate degrades sharply as the failure
rate increases, reaching only $19.8 \pm 5.5$ out of 50 trials at $80\%$
failure, with an elevated average node degree ($13.7$ at $20\%$ failure)
that is symptomatic of the spatial-collapse failure mode reported for
GNN-based methods in the literature (Section~\ref{sec:related-work}).

\subsection{Subnetwork-Level Rigid Translation (SAC)}
\label{sec:sac}

\subsubsection{Architecture and Training}
\textbf{SAC} (Subnetwork-level Adaptive Convolution) partitions surviving
UAVs into subnetworks by connected component under the communication-range
graph, computes one pooled embedding per subnetwork (centroid position and
relative size), and applies a graph convolution over a $k$-nearest-neighbor
graph of subnetwork \emph{centroids} to predict a single 2D translation
vector per subnetwork. By explicit design choice, all UAVs within a given
subnetwork share the exact same translation velocity for that step (pure
rigid translation, with no per-node local adjustment layered on top). This
isolates the spatial-granularity axis as cleanly as possible: SAC is
identical to GAC in every respect (loss functions, training procedure,
graph convolution backbone hyperparameters) except that its motion
commands are generated, and its message passing occurs, at subnetwork
rather than node granularity.

\subsubsection{Results: Reduced Clustering, Collapsed Recovery Capability}
Table~\ref{tab:gac-sac-random} (columns 4--5) shows the opposite failure
mode: SAC achieves a substantially lower, more uniform average node degree
than GAC at every failure rate (e.g., $6.8$ vs.\ $13.7$ at $20\%$ failure),
confirming that subnetwork-level rigid translation does mitigate spatial
collapse. However, SAC's connectivity recovery success rate collapses even
faster than GAC's as the failure rate increases, reaching $5.0 \pm 0.0$ out
of 50 at $60\%$ failure and $0.0 \pm 0.0$ at $80\%$ failure -- a
deterministic failure (zero variance across all 5 seeds), not a rare or
stochastic event. Rigid translation cannot compensate for large numbers of
small, widely separated subnetworks, which the pure global average node
degree metric cannot express: a subnetwork of one node still translates as
a rigid body.

\subsection{Takeaway: Complementary Failure Modes Motivate Hybridization}
\label{sec:takeaway}

GAC and SAC fail in opposite, complementary ways: GAC recovers connectivity
more often but at the cost of severe spatial clustering; SAC preserves a
uniform spatial distribution but cannot reliably reconnect fragmented
subnetworks under moderate-to-severe failure. Neither failure mode is fixed
by more training or a different random seed -- both are architectural
properties of operating at a single, fixed spatial granularity with a
purely learned decision paradigm. This observation directly motivates
hybridizing a learned branch with a heuristic branch (Section
\ref{sec:method}), and later motivates asking \emph{which} granularity the
learned branch in such a hybrid should operate at
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\section{Method: Hybrid Adaptive Convolution (HAC)}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview and Design Principles}
HAC is deliberately designed to be the simplest possible instantiation of
the \emph{hybrid} $\times$ \emph{global} cell in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}:
a parameter-free heuristic branch, identical in form to R2C's ISE module,
fused at the \emph{velocity} level with a single global graph convolution
branch -- explicitly without R2C's subnetwork partitioning, convex-hull
boundary-node extraction, or multipartite graph construction. This
minimalism is intentional: it lets us attribute any performance gap between
HAC and R2C (Section~\ref{sec:r2c-comparison}) specifically to the
architectural machinery HAC omits, rather than to confounded differences in
loss design, training procedure, or hyperparameters.

\subsection{Heuristic Branch: Buffered Virtual-Force Field}
\label{sec:heuristic-branch}

\subsubsection{Three-Zone Force Model}
We reproduce R2C's buffered virtual-spring force field
\citep{rychlewski1984hooke} without modification. For an ideal
inter-UAV distance range $[d_l, d_u] \subset (0, R_c]$, the force on UAV
$v_i$ from a neighbor $v_j$ within communication range is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}'_{ij,t} =
  \begin{cases}
    -\exp\!\left(-\alpha \cdot \dfrac{d_{ij,t}}{d_l}\right) \mathbf{u}_{ij,t}, & 0 \le d_{ij,t} < d_l \\[6pt]
    \mathbf{0}, & d_l \le d_{ij,t} \le d_u \\[4pt]
    \exp\!\left(-\beta \cdot \dfrac{R_c - d_{ij,t}}{R_c - d_u}\right) \mathbf{u}_{ij,t}, & d_u < d_{ij,t} \le R_c,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:heuristic-force}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u}_{ij,t} = (\mathbf{p}_{j,t} - \mathbf{p}_{i,t}) / d_{ij,t}$
is the unit vector from $v_i$ toward $v_j$, and $\alpha, \beta > 0$ control
the decay rates of the repulsive and attractive zones respectively. We use
$\alpha = \beta = 0.01$ and $[d_l, d_u] = [0.8 R_c, 0.9 R_c]$, matching
R2C's reported parameter settings.

\subsubsection{Boundary Repulsion}
To keep UAVs within the deployment area $\mathcal{A}$, we add an inward
boundary-repulsion term for any UAV within a buffer distance $d_e$ of a
mission-area edge $k \in \mathcal{B} = \{\text{left,right,bottom,top}\}$:
$\mathbf{F}''_{i,t} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{B}} \exp(-\delta \cdot d_{i,k})
\, \mathbf{n}_k$, where $d_{i,k}$ is the distance to boundary $k$ and
$\mathbf{n}_k$ its inward normal. The total heuristic force is
$\mathbf{F}_{i,t} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} \mathbf{F}'_{ij,t} +
\mathbf{F}''_{i,t}$ over neighbors $\mathcal{N}_i$ within communication
range, and the resulting velocity is
$\mathbf{v}'_{i,t} = \hat{v}_{\max} \cdot \mathbf{F}_{i,t} /
(\|\mathbf{F}_{i,t}\| + \epsilon)$. Because Eq.~\eqref{eq:heuristic-force}
contains no learnable parameters, this branch requires no training and is
identical at training and evaluation time -- a property we exploit directly
in the theoretical analysis of Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\subsection{Learned Branch: Global Graph Convolution}
\label{sec:learned-branch}

\subsubsection{$k$-Nearest-Neighbor Graph Construction}
At each step, we build a symmetric $k$-nearest-neighbor graph over the
Euclidean positions of \emph{all} surviving UAVs, irrespective of
communication range, so that the graph convolution can propagate
information across otherwise-disconnected subnetworks. We use $k=8$
throughout.

\subsubsection{Node Features and Architecture}
Each node's input feature is a 3-dimensional vector: its normalized $(x,y)$
position and a normalized local degree (computed from the $k$-NN graph).
These are passed through an $L$-layer graph convolutional network (we use
$L=4$, hidden dimension 128) with ReLU activations, followed by a two-layer
MLP head that outputs a 2D direction, passed through $\tanh$ and scaled by
$\hat{v}_{\max}$ to produce $\mathbf{v}''_{i,t}$.

\subsection{Velocity-Level Adaptive Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

\subsubsection{Sigmoid Schedule}
Following R2C's Eq.~22, we define a time-dependent fusion weight
\begin{equation}
  w_T(t) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-k \cdot (t/T - s_0))}, \qquad w_E(t) = 1 - w_T(t),
  \label{eq:awf-schedule}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the recovery horizon, $k > 0$ controls the steepness of the
transition, and $s_0 \in (0,1)$ its center. R2C reports $k=30$, $s_0=0.125$;
we treat both as tunable and study their effect systematically in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\subsubsection{Fusion Formula}
The two branches' velocities are combined and renormalized to magnitude
$\hat{v}_{\max}$, mirroring R2C's Eq.~23:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i,t} = \hat{v}_{\max} \cdot
  \frac{w_E(t)\,\mathbf{v}'_{i,t} + w_T(t)\,\mathbf{v}''_{i,t}}
       {\left\| w_E(t)\,\mathbf{v}'_{i,t} + w_T(t)\,\mathbf{v}''_{i,t} \right\| + \epsilon}.
  \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
Two fixed-weight variants are used throughout as controls:
\textbf{HAC-Fixed} sets $w_E = w_T = 0.5$ for all $t$ (R2C's own
\emph{w/o AWF} ablation design), while \textbf{HAC-Adaptive} uses
Eq.~\eqref{eq:awf-schedule}.

\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

\subsubsection{Connectivity Surrogate Loss}
The number of connected components used in R2C's own connectivity loss
(their Eq.~19) is not differentiable. We instead use a soft-minimum
surrogate: for every pair of currently-disconnected components $S_i, S_j$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{conn}}^{(i,j)} =
  -\tau \left( \log \sum_{\substack{u \in S_i \\ v \in S_j}}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{uv}}{\tau}\right) - \log(n_i n_j) \right),
  \label{eq:conn-loss}
\end{equation}
averaged over all component pairs, where $n_i = |S_i|$, $n_j = |S_j|$. The
$\log(n_i n_j)$ correction term is necessary: without it, a plain
log-sum-exp soft-minimum is not count-invariant, and systematically
under-penalizes distance between large components purely because more
pairs are summed over (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations} documents this bug and
its numerical verification).

\subsubsection{Coverage and Movement Losses}
Coverage is approximated by sampling grid points $\mathbf{g} \in G$ from
the mission area and using a smooth indicator of coverage,
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cov}} = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{\mathbf{g} \in G}
\left(1 - \sigma\!\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{cov}} - \min_i \|\mathbf{g} -
\mathbf{p}_i\|}{\tau'}\right)\right)$, and movement cost by
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{move}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_i -
\mathbf{p}_i\|$. For HAC, both connectivity and coverage losses are
weighted equally ($0.5$ each) throughout training; the connectivity--coverage
trade-off is realized entirely at the \emph{velocity} level via
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}, not by additionally re-weighting the loss -- the key
architectural difference from a single-branch policy with adaptively
weighted loss terms (which we test as a separate ablation in
Section~\ref{sec:motivating}-adjacent experiments, see
Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).

\subsection{Algorithm Summary}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:hac-train} summarizes HAC's training procedure (a
differentiable multi-step rollout with backpropagation through the fused
velocity field), and Algorithm~\ref{alg:hac-deploy} summarizes deployment
(inference-time rollout, no gradient computation).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{HAC Training (one epoch)}
\label{alg:hac-train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Learned policy $\pi_\theta$, rollout length $T$, weight schedule $w_T(\cdot)$
\State Sample failure ratio $r \sim \{0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$; initialize positions $\mathbf{P}_0$; apply failure
\State $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} \gets 0$
\For{$t = 0, \dots, T-1$}
  \State Compute connected components $\{S_k\}$ of $\mathbf{P}_t$ under $R_c$
  \State $\mathbf{v}' \gets$ heuristic velocities via Eq.~\eqref{eq:heuristic-force} \Comment{no gradient}
  \State Build $k$-NN graph over $\mathbf{P}_t$; $\mathbf{v}'' \gets \pi_\theta(\mathbf{P}_t)$ \Comment{learned branch}
  \State $w_T \gets w_T(t)$; $w_E \gets 1 - w_T$
  \State $\hat{\mathbf{v}} \gets$ fuse $(\mathbf{v}', \mathbf{v}'', w_E, w_T)$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}
  \State $\mathbf{P}_{t+1} \gets \text{clip}(\mathbf{P}_t + \Delta t \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}, \; 0, \; L)$
  \State $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} \gets \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{conn}}(\mathbf{P}_{t+1}) + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cov}}(\mathbf{P}_{t+1}) + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{move}}$
\EndFor
\State Backpropagate $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} / T$ through $\pi_\theta$; update $\theta$ via Adam
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{HAC Deployment (inference rollout)}
\label{alg:hac-deploy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Trained policy $\pi_\theta$, recovery horizon $T$, weight schedule $w_T(\cdot)$
\For{$t = 0, \dots, T-1$}
  \If{swarm is fully connected} \textbf{return} success, $t$
  \EndIf
  \State $\mathbf{v}' \gets$ heuristic velocities via Eq.~\eqref{eq:heuristic-force}
  \State $\mathbf{v}'' \gets \pi_\theta(\mathbf{P}_t)$ \Comment{no gradient}
  \State $\hat{\mathbf{v}} \gets$ fuse $(\mathbf{v}', \mathbf{v}'', 1-w_T(t), w_T(t))$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}
  \State $\mathbf{P}_{t+1} \gets \text{clip}(\mathbf{P}_t + \Delta t \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}, \; 0, \; L)$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} failure (horizon exhausted)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

Section~\ref{sec:ablation} will show empirically that naively porting
R2C's fusion schedule (Eq.~\eqref{eq:awf-schedule} with $k=30$, $s_0=0.125$)
to HAC \emph{degrades} robustness relative to a fixed $50/50$ fusion, and
that smoothing the schedule (reducing $k$) largely restores it. This
section gives two complementary explanations for why the steepness of the
transition, rather than its timing, is the operative variable: a
test-time argument based on a monotonicity property of the heuristic branch
(Section~\ref{sec:monotonicity}), and a training-time argument based on the
schedule's role as an implicit curriculum for the learned branch
(Section~\ref{sec:curriculum}), which we identify as the more fundamental
of the two.

\subsection{Local Monotonicity of the Heuristic Branch}
\label{sec:monotonicity}

\begin{proposition}[Attractive-zone descent guarantee]
\label{prop:monotonicity}
Let $v_i, v_j$ be two UAVs with $d_u < d_{ij,t} \le R_c$ (i.e., in the
attractive zone of Eq.~\eqref{eq:heuristic-force}), and suppose no other
neighbor's repulsive or boundary force acts on $v_i$ or $v_j$. Then, for
sufficiently small $\Delta t$, applying the heuristic branch alone
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:heuristic-force} with $w_E = 1, w_T = 0$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}) strictly decreases $d_{ij,t}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Eq.~\eqref{eq:heuristic-force}, the pairwise force on $v_i$ from $v_j$ in
the attractive zone is
$\mathbf{F}'_{ij,t} = \exp\!\left(-\beta \frac{R_c - d_{ij,t}}{R_c-d_u}\right)
\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}$ with $\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}$ pointing from $v_i$ toward
$v_j$; by symmetry, $\mathbf{F}'_{ji,t} = -\mathbf{F}'_{ij,t}\cdot
(d_{ij,t}/d_{ji,t}) = -\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}\exp(\cdot)$ points from $v_j$
toward $v_i$ with equal magnitude (since $d_{ij,t}=d_{ji,t}$ and
$\mathbf{u}_{ji,t}=-\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}$). Under the stated isolation
assumption, $\mathbf{F}_{i,t} = \mathbf{F}'_{ij,t}$ and $\mathbf{F}_{j,t} =
\mathbf{F}'_{ji,t} = -\mathbf{F}'_{ij,t}$, so
$\mathbf{v}'_{i,t} = \hat{v}_{\max}\,\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}$ and
$\mathbf{v}'_{j,t} = -\hat{v}_{\max}\,\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}$ (the normalization
in $\mathbf{v}'_{i,t} = \hat{v}_{\max}\mathbf{F}_{i,t}/(\|\mathbf{F}_{i,t}\|+
\epsilon)$ preserves direction). The one-step position update gives
$d_{ij,t+\Delta t} = \|\mathbf{p}_{i,t} + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}'_{i,t} -
\mathbf{p}_{j,t} - \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}'_{j,t}\| = \|(\mathbf{p}_{i,t} -
\mathbf{p}_{j,t}) - 2\Delta t\, \hat{v}_{\max}\, \mathbf{u}_{ij,t}\|$.
Since $\mathbf{p}_{i,t}-\mathbf{p}_{j,t} = -d_{ij,t}\,\mathbf{u}_{ij,t}$,
this equals $|{-d_{ij,t}} - 2\Delta t\, \hat{v}_{\max}| = d_{ij,t} -
2\Delta t \hat v_{\max}$ for $\Delta t$ small enough that this remains
non-negative, which is strictly less than $d_{ij,t}$ for any $\Delta t >
0$. $\blacksquare$
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:monotonicity} gives the heuristic branch a
\emph{local, certifiable descent guarantee} on connectivity, at least
within the attractive zone and modulo interference from other forces. The
learned branch carries no such guarantee -- indeed
Section~\ref{sec:motivating} shows GAC can \emph{increase} average node
degree relative to a more spatially balanced configuration, i.e.\ move in a
direction the connectivity objective does not favor. Because
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion} is a convex combination (before renormalization),
there is intuitively a threshold $w_E^{\min}$ (state- and
training-progress-dependent) below which the learned branch's contribution
can overwhelm the heuristic branch's descent direction. This motivates
asking, for a given trajectory, how large a fraction of the recovery
process operates with $w_E(t)$ below such a threshold -- which the schedule
parameters $k, s_0$ control directly via Eq.~\eqref{eq:awf-schedule}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fusion Weight as an Implicit Training Curriculum}
\label{sec:curriculum}

The monotonicity argument above is a \emph{test-time} account: it explains
why control should not be handed too fully to the learned branch
mid-trajectory. But it does not by itself explain why smoothing the
schedule (as opposed to, say, delaying it) is what fixes the problem
empirically (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). We argue the more fundamental
mechanism is a \emph{training-time} one, exploiting the fact that
$\mathbf{v}''$ in HAC is trained \emph{end-to-end} through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hac-train}), rather than
pretrained in isolation and fused only at deployment.

\begin{definition}[Autonomy exposure]
For a training rollout of length $T_{\mathrm{train}}$ and a threshold
$\tau \in (0,1)$, the \emph{autonomy exposure} of a schedule
$w_T(\cdot; k, s_0)$ is the fraction of training steps at which the
learned branch is in majority control:
$\mathrm{AE}(k, s_0; \tau) = \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{train}}} \bigl|
\{\, t \in \{0,\dots,T_{\mathrm{train}}-1\} : w_T(t; k, s_0) > \tau \,\}
\bigr|$.
\end{definition}

For $\tau = 0.5$, the sigmoid in Eq.~\eqref{eq:awf-schedule} crosses from
$10\%$ to $90\%$ over an interval of width
$\Delta t \approx \frac{\ln(81)}{k} \cdot T_{\mathrm{train}} \approx
\frac{4.394}{k}\,T_{\mathrm{train}}$ (derived from inverting the sigmoid at
the $10\%$/$90\%$ points), so smaller $k$ directly widens the transition
window and increases the training-time exposure the learned branch has to
\emph{low}-$w_T$ regimes, where the heuristic branch is doing most of the
work and the learned branch is effectively performing residual correction
rather than autonomous control.

\begin{remark}[Curriculum interpretation]
A steep schedule (large $k$, e.g.\ R2C's default $k=30$) confronts the
learned branch, for the majority of a training rollout, with a
\emph{harder} learning problem: near-autonomous control with no heuristic
correction, compounding open-loop error over many steps. A smooth schedule
(small $k$) instead keeps the learned branch in a residual-correction
regime for most of training -- a form of curriculum learning by easy-to-hard
ordering, except here the ``easy'' and ``hard'' regimes are not separate
training phases but different \emph{fractions of exposure within every
single rollout}.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Consistency with Observed Data}
This account is consistent with the two strongest empirical facts from
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}: (i) among the schedules we test, the smoothest
($k=5$, transition width $\approx 88\%$ of the $40$-step training rollout)
performs best, nearly matching HAC-Fixed at $80\%$ failure; and (ii) this
fix does \emph{not} transfer to HSC (the subnetwork-level hybrid variant),
whose learned branch is architecturally restricted to per-subnetwork rigid
translation -- no amount of curriculum smoothing can grant a policy
representational capacity it does not have, precisely the boundary
condition the curriculum account predicts.

\subsubsection{An Honest Gap in the Account}
We do not fully explain the observed non-monotonicity between two
intermediate schedules (\S\ref{sec:ablation}: $k=10, s_0=0.125$ outperforms
$k=30, s_0=0.5$, in the direction the curriculum account predicts, but is
not itself clearly better than R2C's default $k=30, s_0=0.125$ despite a
$3\times$ wider transition window). These intermediate points were swept
with a single training seed each; given the five-seed variance we later
measure for comparable architectures (success-count standard deviations up
to $\pm 5$--$6$ out of 50), the ordering among intermediate schedules is
plausibly within noise, and we do not treat it as evidence against the
curriculum account -- nor as evidence for it.

\subsection{A Falsifiable Prediction for Future Work}
\label{sec:prediction}
If the autonomy-exposure account is correct, $\mathrm{AE}(k,s_0;0.5)$
should predict final success-rate ranking \emph{better} than $k$ or $s_0$
individually -- i.e., two schedules with different $(k,s_0)$ but matched
$\mathrm{AE}$ should perform comparably. This is a concrete, computable,
and falsifiable prediction: re-running the schedule sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} with matched autonomy exposure across several
seeds, rather than the single-seed sweep reported there, would directly
test it.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}

\subsection{Simulation Environment}

\subsubsection{Deployment Scenarios}
We evaluate in a $1000 \times 1000\,\mathrm{m}^2$ area under two deployment
scenarios, mirroring \citet{peng2026r2c}: \textbf{random}, with $200$ UAVs
placed uniformly at random, and \textbf{uniform}, with $196$ UAVs arranged
on a $14\times14$ grid. Each UAV has communication and coverage radius
$R_c = R_{\mathrm{cov}} = 120\,\mathrm{m}$, maximum speed
$\hat{v}_{\max} = 1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and a recovery horizon of $T=400$
simulation steps at evaluation time.

\subsubsection{Failure Model and Independent Trials}
We test four failure ratios, $\{20\%, 40\%, 60\%, 80\%\}$, with failed
nodes selected uniformly at random (the paper's own failure-selection
procedure is not specified beyond ``unpredictable random failure
patterns''; see Appendix~\ref{app:deviations} for this and other
documented deviations). For each (architecture, deployment, failure ratio)
combination we run $5$ independent training seeds $\times$ $50$
independent evaluation trials per seed, and report the mean $\pm$ standard
deviation across seeds of each trial-level metric's mean.

\subsection{Baselines}

\subsubsection{Heuristic-Only Baseline}
The virtual-force branch (Section~\ref{sec:heuristic-branch}) run in
isolation ($w_E{=}1, w_T{=}0$ throughout); requires no training.

\subsubsection{Learned Baselines}
GAC and SAC (Section~\ref{sec:motivating}); and a \emph{learned-only}
ablation of HAC itself, in which the fusion weight is forced to
$w_T{=}1$ throughout both training and evaluation (the learned branch
receives zero contribution from the heuristic branch at any point).

\subsubsection{R2C (Non-Reproduced Reference)}
\label{sec:baselines-r2c}
We additionally report R2C's own numbers as published in its Table~1 and
Table~2 \citep{peng2026r2c}. R2C's code is not publicly released; this
comparison is explicitly \emph{not} a controlled reproduction and is
discussed with that caveat in Section~\ref{sec:r2c-comparison}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We report: \textbf{Success/50}, the number of evaluation trials (out of
50) reaching $C_{t} = 1$ within the horizon $T$ (trials that do not are
excluded from the remaining per-trial metrics, following R2C's own
convention of marking non-recovering configurations invalid);
\textbf{REC}, the recovery time ratio $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$ averaged over
successful trials; \textbf{COV}, the coverage ratio $C_{\mathrm{cov}}(t_r)$
at the moment of recovery, averaged over successful trials; and
\textbf{AND}, the average node degree at the moment of recovery, used as a
proxy for spatial clustering (higher AND indicates more clustering, all
else equal).

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\subsubsection{Network and Optimization Hyperparameters}
All graph convolution backbones (GAC, SAC, and HAC's learned branch) use
$L{=}4$ layers, hidden dimension $128$, ReLU activations, and a two-layer
MLP head. Training uses Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, gradient-norm
clipping at $1.0$, and $300$ training epochs, each consisting of a
differentiable rollout of $40$ steps (substantially shorter than the
$400$-step evaluation horizon, a documented tractability trade-off; see
Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\subsubsection{Multi-Seed Protocol}
An early implementation issue left network weight initialization
uncontrolled by the \texttt{--seed} argument (only environment-side
randomness, e.g.\ failure selection, was seeded). We identified and fixed
this prior to running the five-seed studies reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}; Appendix~\ref{app:deviations} documents the fix
and its measurable effect on baseline variance.

subnetwork partitioning.

\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:ablation}

\subsection{Branch Contribution: Heuristic-Only vs.\ Learned-Only vs.\ Fusion}
\label{sec:branch-ablation}

Tables~\ref{tab:main-random} and~\ref{tab:main-uniform} already contain
this ablation: HAC-Fixed (full fusion) against its two constituent branches
run in isolation. At $80\%$ failure (random deployment), the fusion reaches
$48.8\pm0.98$ successes versus $12.0\pm3.5$ (learned-only) and $0.0\pm0.0$
(heuristic-only); at uniform $60\%$ failure, fusion reaches $50.0\pm0.0$
versus $33.8\pm6.4$ (learned-only) and $0.8\pm0.7$ (heuristic-only). Two
observations rule out the two most obvious alternative explanations for
HAC's performance. First, the two branches fail in different, non-nested
failure ranges (heuristic-only degrades earliest, learned-only more
gradually but from a lower ceiling), so fusion cannot be explained as
``mostly using whichever branch is better.'' Second, fusion's success rate
at high failure exceeds \emph{both} branches' individual success rates by
a wide margin, not merely the better of the two -- direct evidence of a
genuine synergistic effect rather than redundancy.

\subsection{Adaptive vs.\ Fixed Fusion Weighting}
\label{sec:adaptive-ablation}

\subsubsection{Default Schedule Underperforms Fixed Weighting}
Applying R2C's reported schedule ($k=30$, $s_0=0.125$) to HAC-Adaptive
\emph{reverses} the direction of R2C's own \emph{w/o AWF} ablation: at
$80\%$ failure (random deployment, single seed), HAC-Adaptive reaches only
$20/50$ successes versus HAC-Fixed's $48.8\pm0.98$ (Table
\ref{tab:main-random}).

\subsection{Granularity Substitution: HSC as a Counter-Experiment}
\label{sec:hsc}

\subsubsection{Experimental Design}
To test whether schedule smoothing is a general fix for adaptive fusion or
specific to HAC's global learned branch, we construct \textbf{HSC}: the
same heuristic branch and the same Eq.~\eqref{eq:awf-schedule}/
\eqref{eq:fusion} fusion mechanism as HAC, but with the learned branch
replaced by SAC's subnetwork-level rigid-translation policy
(Section~\ref{sec:sac}) -- architecturally, the combination closest to
R2C's own ISE+IST+AWF design among those we test.

$80\%$ failure, vs.\ $0/50$ for the default schedule in both cases).

\subsubsection{Interpretation}
This cleanly separates two distinct failure modes that could otherwise be

express the fine-grained corrections needed under severe fragmentation, no
matter how gently or gradually it is granted control. No curriculum can
teach a model to compute an output outside its own output space.

\subsection{Reproducibility Fix: Seed Control and Its Effect on Baseline Variance}
\label{sec:seed-fix}

During this study we discovered that our training scripts'
\texttt{--seed} argument controlled only environment-side randomness
(failure selection, initial deployment), leaving PyTorch's network weight
initialization unseeded. A single early GAC-Fixed run (uncontrolled
initialization) reported $20\%$-failure COV of $0.830$ and AND of $11.87$;
after fixing weight-initialization seeding and averaging over five properly
seeded runs, the same configuration measures COV $0.747\pm0.009$ and AND
$13.68\pm0.28$ (Table~\ref{tab:main-random}) -- a shift larger than one
standard deviation, indicating the original single run was an
optimistically-biased draw rather than a representative one. We report
this as a concrete illustration of why the seeding fix and the subsequent
five-seed protocol (Section~\ref{sec:expsetup}) were necessary before
drawing architecture-level conclusions, and log it in full in
Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Simple Hybrids Work: Complementary Failure Modes Revisited}
The through-line connecting Sections~\ref{sec:motivating},
\ref{sec:results}, and~\ref{sec:branch-ablation} is that GAC's and SAC's
failure modes are complementary at the level of \emph{individual
trajectories}, not merely on average: the heuristic branch provides a
locally certifiable descent direction on connectivity
(Proposition~\ref{prop:monotonicity}) whenever it is in the attractive
zone, and the learned branch's global receptive field lets it act on
information no purely local rule can see (e.g., the relative position of a
distant, disconnected component). Neither alone is sufficient, but their
weaknesses do not overlap in the same states at the same time, which is
precisely the condition under which a fusion -- even a naively simple,
fixed-weight one -- outperforms either constituent.

\subsection{When Schedule Smoothing Helps and When It Doesn't}
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows this benefit is conditional: schedule
smoothing rescues HAC (whose learned branch has per-node representational
capacity) but not HSC (whose learned branch is restricted to per-subnetwork
translation). We read this as a general design principle for adaptive
fusion of heuristic and learned components: \emph{before} tuning the
fusion schedule, verify that the learned branch's output space is rich
enough to express the corrections the task demands at the granularity the
fusion schedule will eventually hand it full control over. Tuning a
schedule cannot substitute for representational capacity the architecture
does not have.

\subsection{Limitations}

\subsubsection{Simplified Learned Branch}
HAC's global graph convolution omits R2C's convex-hull boundary-node
extraction, virtual cross-subgraph edge construction, and larger
($8$-layer, $512$-hidden) spectral convolution. This is by design (Section
\ref{sec:method}), but it means the remaining $9$--$11$ percentage-point
coverage gap at $80\%$ failure (Section~\ref{sec:r2c-comparison}) cannot
currently be closed without reintroducing some of this machinery, and we
have not yet identified which specific piece (if any) is responsible for
the gap.

\subsubsection{Non-Controlled Comparison with R2C}
R2C's code is not publicly released. Our comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:r2c-comparison} uses numbers as published, under an
independently re-implemented simulation environment; low-failure-rate
comparisons in particular should be read as indicative rather than
precise, per the discussion in Section~\ref{sec:r2c-comparison}.

\subsubsection{Truncated Training Horizon}
All models are trained on $40$-step differentiable rollouts but evaluated
on the full $400$-step recovery horizon, a tractability trade-off for
backpropagation-through-time training. We have not measured whether
longer training rollouts would change any of the reported comparisons.

\subsubsection{Single-Seed Schedule and HSC Sweeps}
The schedule steepness sweep (Table~\ref{tab:schedule-sweep}) and the HSC
counter-experiment (Table~\ref{tab:hsc}) are each run with a single
training seed per configuration, unlike the five-seed main results
(Tables~\ref{tab:main-random}--\ref{tab:main-uniform}). Given the
five-seed variance measured for comparable architectures (success-count
standard deviations up to $\pm 5$--$6$ out of $50$), fine-grained orderings
within these sweeps -- but not their headline conclusions, which are
corroborated by cross-deployment verification and by the theoretical
account of Section~\ref{sec:theory} -- should be treated as preliminary.

\subsection{Future Work}
Three directions follow directly from the limitations above: (i) test the
falsifiable autonomy-exposure prediction of
Section~\ref{sec:prediction} with a multi-seed schedule sweep; (ii)
identify, via targeted ablation of R2C's IST machinery, which specific
component (boundary-node extraction vs.\ multipartite edges vs.\ network
capacity) is responsible for R2C's remaining coverage advantage; and (iii)
explore lightweight ways to grant a subnetwork-level learned branch
node-level expressiveness -- e.g., a small per-node residual on top of the
subnetwork translation -- to test whether this closes HSC's gap without
reverting to a fully global learned branch.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We set out to answer two questions R2C's own ablations leave open: does
its subnetwork-level learned component need to be subnetwork-level, and
does its adaptive fusion schedule need its specific reported parameters?
Through a sequence of controlled experiments -- motivating single-branch
failure modes, a deliberately minimal hybrid architecture (HAC), a
schedule-steepness sweep, and a granularity-substitution counter-experiment
(HSC) -- we find that the answer to both questions is more nuanced than
either ``yes'' or ``no.'' A single global learned branch, fused with a
parameter-free heuristic branch under fixed weighting, already recovers
the large majority of R2C's benefit with markedly higher robustness than
any single-branch alternative we test. R2C's adaptive schedule, however,
does not transfer as-is to this simpler architecture; the failure is one
of transition steepness rather than adaptive weighting per se, is fixable
by smoothing the schedule, and -- critically -- that fix is itself
architecture-dependent, transferring to a node-level learned branch but
not to a subnetwork-level rigid-translation one. We report this account,
including a falsified hypothesis, a documented numerical bug, and several
single-seed results explicitly flagged as preliminary, in the interest of
giving future work an honest and reproducible starting point rather than a
polished but incomplete one.

\section{Deviations Log}
\label{app:deviations}

We log every point at which our implementation deviates from
\citet{peng2026r2c}'s stated methodology, or from our own earlier
implementation, in the interest of full transparency about what was
actually measured.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Failure-node selection.} R2C states only ``unpredictable
  random failure patterns'' without specifying the selection algorithm; we
  use uniform random node removal.
  \item \textbf{Connectivity loss surrogate.} R2C's own connectivity loss
  (its Eq.~19) counts connected components, which is non-differentiable.
  We use the soft-minimum surrogate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:conn-loss}.
  \item \textbf{Count-bias bug and fix.} An earlier version of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:conn-loss} omitted the $\log(n_i n_j)$ correction term.
  Because plain log-sum-exp is not count-invariant, this systematically
  under-penalized distance between large disconnected components
  independent of their true spatial separation, occasionally producing
  spuriously negative training losses. We verified the fix numerically: on
  a controlled synthetic case with matched true minimum distance
  ($\approx 100$--$108\,\mathrm{m}$) but mismatched pair counts ($3\times3$
  vs.\ $30\times30$), the uncorrected estimator returned $2.85$ and
  $-230.58$ respectively (wildly count-dependent), while the corrected
  estimator returned $112.71$ and $109.54$ (both close to the true
  minimum, as intended). All results in this paper use the corrected
  estimator; results computed before the fix are not reported.
  \item \textbf{Boundary buffer width $d_e$.} Not numerically specified by
  R2C; we use $d_e = 0.15 R_c$, the same order of magnitude as the neutral
  zone width $d_u - d_l = 0.1 R_c$.
  \item \textbf{Truncated training rollout.} Training uses $40$-step
  rollouts versus the full $400$-step evaluation horizon, for
  backpropagation-through-time tractability (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
  \item \textbf{Coverage computed via Monte Carlo grid sampling}, at
  $5\,\mathrm{m}$ resolution, rather than R2C's (unspecified) exact
  implementation.
  \item \textbf{Weight-initialization seeding bug and fix.} Documented in
  Section~\ref{sec:seed-fix}; the \texttt{--seed} argument originally
  controlled only environment-side randomness. Fixed by explicitly calling
  \texttt{torch.manual\_seed} and \texttt{torch.cuda.manual\_seed\_all}
  before model construction in every training script, prior to the
  five-seed studies reported in this paper.
  \item \textbf{R2C code unavailable.} No official implementation is
  released; Section~\ref{sec:r2c-comparison}'s comparison uses R2C's
  published numbers directly and is explicitly not a controlled
  reproduction.
\end{itemize}
